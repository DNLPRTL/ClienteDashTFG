ABSTRACT Adaptive video streaming offers enhanced Quality of Experience (QoE) by dynamically adjusting the video quality to match network conditions and device capabilities. However, employing effective video streaming systems is becoming more challenging as user demands for high quality and low latency grow. The surge in video traffic is not only straining network resources but also causing a decline in video quality. To address these challenges, machine learning algorithms leverage data-driven techniques to optimize video delivery, improve QoE, and reduce network congestion. Although several surveys exist on the role of machine learning in adaptive streaming, there remains a lack of an up-to- date review that comprehensively covers the use of machine learning throughout all the stages of video streaming and explores practical deployment challenges and opportunities. This survey addresses this gap by systematically categorizing and analyzing recent research on learning techniques applied to adaptive video encoding, bandwidth optimization, and quality adaptation. Emerging trends and open challenges are identified, providing researchers with timely insights into how learning algorithms can be used to shape the future of adaptive streaming systems. 1) ENERGY EFFICIENCY IN VIDEO STREAMING SYSTEMS
Energy consumption is a growing concern across the

different screen resolutions. Video encoding therefore needs

latency, high-throughput service. To address these issues,
machine learning (ML) techniques are being proposed for
energy-aware video encoding and quality adaptation, aiming
to improve energy usage without degrading user experience.

Furthermore, adaptive video streaming, while providing
improved quality and user experience, can contribute to
excessive bandwidth consumption and video-related traffic.
Video-related traffic suffers from congestion, high costs,
and high energy consumption [5], among other challenges.
Therefore, it is crucial to develop methods for video traffic
optimization.

bandwidth usage; inefficient quality adaptation algorithms
do not account for content characteristics and greedily
select higher bitrates that do not always result in substantial
quality improvements. Additionally, available computing
resources at the client or server side are often under-utilized,
increasing the pressure on the network. Learning-based

by optimizing traffic and resource utilization. Using historical
data, learning-based techniques can also be used to accurately
predict network conditions [7], [8], [9] to further improve
quality adaptation decisions.

remains a central challenge in video streaming. Since QoE
is used to guide the quality adaptation algorithm, building
an accurate QoE model is extensively researched within
the literature [10]. However, quantifying the user’s viewing
experience in itself is a challenging task. Perception of
different users is influenced by their preferences in terms of
both QoE and content. Although subjective quality measures,
such as Mean Opinion Score (MOS), are considered the

cannot be used for real-time QoE measurement. Rather, QoE

and disregards user preferences. This can result in poorer
viewing experience as users may prioritize different aspects
or metrics of QoE [12]. As a result, ML is increasingly being
used to model and predict actual perceived QoE, allowing for
content-aware and user-centric adaptation.

Traditional
hybrid

standards are based on block-based hybrid architectures that
include modules for intra/inter-frame prediction, exploiting
the spatial and temporal redundancies of video content,
transform coding, quantization, and entropy coding. How-
ever, while highly optimized and standardized, these systems
face growing limitations, particularly with the advent of
4K/8K video. These systems do not learn from data and
must be manually tuned for each content type, resolution,
or bitrate range. In addition, encoding decisions rely on
fixed heuristics, which do not capture semantic or perceptual
importance of video content. With each new standard,
compression gains also become harder to achieve without
increasing the complexity, making real-time encoding more
expensive [26], [27].
Consequently, machine learning techniques have increas-
ingly been suggested to address the challenges of traditional
encoding frameworks. Learning-based, or learned, video
coding frameworks have the potential to provide enhanced
capabilities, such as content-aware encoding [28], reduced
computational complexity [29], and end-to-end optimization
of encoding pipelines. However, despite their promising
performance, integrating learned video coding frameworks
into existing adaptive streaming systems presents several
challenges. These models often have high computational and
memory requirements, making them unable to achieve real-
time encoding performance [27], particularly in the case of
neural network-based schemes. The lack of standardization
and publicly available benchmarks and datasets for learned
codecs also limits their widespread adoption and hinders
research efforts. Standardized testbeds and lightweight
learned codecs are therefore needed to transition these
advances into production-level adaptive streaming systems.
Several learned codecs have been proposed to address
these limitations. Notable examples of such frameworks
include DVC [30], which is the first end-to-end neural-
enhanced encoding algorithm, now used as a benchmark
for other studies in the field. Reference [31] presents a
comparative benchmarking study of several learned video
coding algorithms, including DVC, SSF [32], DCVC [33],
and DVC-P [34]. The study shows that DCVC achieves
the best rate-distortion performance at the cost of having
the highest complexity, whereas SSF achieves the lowest
GPU memory occupancy and fastest performance with the
second-best rate-distortion performance. Similarly, Google
DeepMind’s C3 [35] is another learned video coding scheme
that targets complexity, achieving significantly lower decod-
ing complexity compared to similar learned frameworks by
overfitting a smaller, less complex model to each video rather
than developing a generalized encoding model.
As research continues to improve the efficiency of
video encoding, another critical component of the encoding
process, bitrate ladder construction, has also seen significant
advancements through machine learning techniques. In
adaptive video streaming, videos are encoded at multiple
resolutions and bitrates. The resolution and bitrate pairs,
which make up the bitrate ladder, are selected such that users
can receive the best possible viewing experience given their
device specifications and network conditions. Commonly,
rate-distortion (RD) curves illustrate the relationship between
the bitrate and the quality for each encoded resolution of a
specific video sequence. The RD curve for each resolution
peaks at a certain bitrate range, in which this resolution will
have the highest quality out of all the possible resolutions.
Joining these peaks results in the construction of a convex
hull, which indicates the ideal resolution-bitrate pairs as
depicted in Fig. 3. Constructing the bitrate ladder then
involves determining the cross-points along the RD curves.
These cross-points are the points along the convex hull
at which the current resolution must switch to the next
resolution.

B. PER-SCENE ENCODING
Despite the improvement of per-title approaches over tradi-
tional encoding schemes, generating a single bitrate ladder
for the entire video sequence may still lead to degraded
perceptual quality and bandwidth inefficiency, especially
in videos with dynamic scene changes. This is because
high complexity scenes require higher bitrate allocation
as compared to low complexity scenes, which can be
represented at an acceptable visual quality using less bits.
Consequently, using per-title encoding may not only lead
to sub-optimal visual quality but to inefficient bandwidth
utilization, as well.
Per-scene or per-chunk encoding are the main alternatives
to per-title encoding introduced in the literature [54], [55],
[56]. Per-scene encoding involves predicting the bitrate
ladder for each video scene, which consists of one or
more segments. Each video scene consists of a duration of
the video throughout which there is no significant change
in content complexity. Therefore, encoding the video on
a per-scene basis results in improved visual quality and
bandwidth efficiency. Such encoding approaches are now
being practically implemented by some video streaming
services, such as Netflix’s per-shot encoder [57]. However,
an exhaustive approach is still commonly used for bitrate
ladder generation, which is addressed in recent research
through the use of machine learning.

requires scene detection and analysis, which is approached

C. PER-CHUNK ENCODING
An alternative approach to per-title and per-scene methods,
per-chunk encoding, involves bitrate ladder prediction on a
per-chunk, or per-segment, basis, where each chunk is simply
a pre-determined, fixed duration of video, independent of the
type of video content. Per-chunk encoding is based on the
assumption that each video segment generally has the same
content complexity throughout its duration, due to segments
usually being less than 10 seconds long. As such, many
works [65] tend to choose per-chunk encoding over per-
scene encoding, in order to skip the scene detection step. One
such approach in [55] also attempts to simplify the content
analysis step. It uses a deep reinforcement learning (DRL)
framework for per-chunk bitrate ladder prediction based on
video content, network capacity, and storage cost (refer to
Table 1 for a brief explanation of relevant machine learning
methods). However, the content of each segment is analyzed
by examining only the I-frame, which may not necessarily
be representative of the entire segment, particularly if longer
segment durations are used. Another recent work on per-
chunk encoding is proposed in [66]. The authors design a
deep neural network that predicts the CRF for a given target
VMAF value based on the video segment features.
In the case of live streaming, low-latency coding
approaches are required to maintain high QoE. As such,
encoding efficiency can be sacrificed for speed; for example,
one-pass encoding is often used rather than two-pass
encoding. In one-pass encoding, the encoder processes the
content in one ‘‘pass’’, quickly allocating bitrate based on
information about the current and past frames. Two-pass
encoding, on the other hand, involves an initial pass to analyze
video content over the whole duration, then a second pass
for bitrate allocation, making it more efficient but slower.
Intuitively, one-pass encoding is more suitable to meet the
low-latency requirements of live streaming, meaning that live
streams are often encoded inefficiently. A recent work that
successfully addresses this problem presents ETPS [39], a
two-pass encoding scheme for live streaming that improves
compression efficiency while maintaining the required low
latency. ETPS uses the spatial and temporal complexity
based on the DCT energy of the first GOP of a segment
to determine the optimized CRF. Other approaches similarly
attempt to improve the process of live video coding by taking
advantage of other encoding parameters. In order to meet
stringent latency and quality requirements, these approaches
optimize the video encoding configuration. The authors
in [67] analyze the relationship between various encoding
parameters (such as the number of frames per second and
encoding speed) and video perceptual quality; they find that
varying the encoding configuration rather than just adjusting
the resolution or bitrate can achieve acceptable levels of both
latency and video quality. Based on their analysis, they design
a reinforcement learning (RL)-based scheme for optimizing
encoding configuration in real-time video streaming with a
client-side super-resolution algorithm to further improve the
video quality.

sub-optimal bitrate usage and perceptual quality if per-chunk
encoding is used.
III. BANDWIDTH OPTIMIZATION
Video-related traffic constitutes the majority of Internet
traffic, and as a result, the growing demand for ultra-high-
definition (UHD) video and the advent of 360◦streaming
has led to increased bandwidth requirements. Consequently,
efficiently utilizing the available bandwidth has become
one of the core challenges for video streaming. Traditional
approaches to video streaming, often relying on fixed
rules, fail to adapt to the variability of network conditions.
Consequently, substantial data wastage occurs during periods
of ample bandwidth, while sub-optimal quality during
network congestion reduces user satisfaction. To address this
issue, learning techniques have emerged as an alternative
approach to intelligently utilize available network bandwidth
and reduce data wastage. Their ability to learn from historical
patterns and adapt in real-time enables them to tailor their
actions to varying network conditions. This section delves
into the use of learning-based methods to optimize video
streaming traffic without compromising perceptual quality.
A. INTELLIGENT ADAPTATION AND ENCODING
1) PERCEPTUALLY-AWARE QUALITY ADAPTATION
In adaptive streaming, videos are encoded at multiple bitrates
which make up the bitrate ladder. A lower bitrate generally
corresponds to lower video quality. However, increasing the
bitrate does not necessarily mean increasing the quality;
while the quality increases linearly for a specific range of
bitrates, increasing the bitrate further outside the linear range
does not have a noticeable effect on video quality [68]. In typ-
ical adaptive bitrate algorithms, higher bitrates are selected
greedily, increasing energy and data consumption without any
perceivable increase in quality. Turkkan et al. [68] design a
deep Q-learning (DQL)-based ABR algorithm, GreenABR,
to address this. Their framework reduces data consumption
due to video streaming while maintaining high QoE by
adding an energy penalty to the learning model’s reward.
As a result, GreenABR manages to significantly improve
bandwidth efficiency and reduce energy consumption com-
pared to state-of-the-art algorithms such as Pensieve [16]
while achieving comparable QoE. Other research [69] utilizes
measures such as VMAF to ensure the selection of bitrates
that result in an appreciable improvement in quality. Such
perceptual quality-aware streaming approaches ensure that
high bitrates are not unnecessarily selected without any
visible visual improvement.
2) BUFFER-AWARE QUALITY ADAPTATION

targeted to optimize bandwidth usage is the buffer occupancy.

decision. Greedily requesting video chunks and filling up the
playback buffer can result in inefficient bandwidth usage.

buffering and improve the QoE, it can lead to data wastage if
the user stops watching. This is addressed in Qian et al.’s work
on DAM [70]. DAM is a short video streaming framework
for reducing data wastage due to pre-fetching. Its DRL-based
ABR algorithm is responsible for selecting the video chunk

3) JND-BASED ENCODING
A similar concept can be applied during the video encoding

coding and bitrate ladder construction. Here, we also provide
a brief overview of recent works on video coding which are
targeted toward reducing bandwidth wastage. These works
make use of several methods to achieve their goal, such as
the Just Noticeable Difference (JND). JND is a quality metric
that measures the minimum visual perceivable difference by
the human eye. Some research [75] is dedicated to JND
prediction, which presents another challenge on its own.
Using encoding ladders with no perceivable visual difference
between each quality level leads to data wastage. As such,
recent works aim to design JND-aware encoding algorithms.
The authors in [76] propose an ML-based per-title
encoding scheme based on JND. This framework uses
support vector regression to predict the JND and generates
an encoding ladder with constant JND intervals. Similarly,
another model, MCBE [77], aims to reduce the energy
consumption of adaptive video streaming by optimizing the
bitrate ladders of different video codecs. It employs the ran-

by the VMAF score, of different bitrate-resolution pairs and
eliminates the redundant representations that do not enhance
perceived video quality beyond a certain JND threshold.
Another JND-based scheme is ViSOR [78], a video super-
resolution-aware online scheme that takes advantage of client
computing capabilities to reduce the energy consumption of
the encoding process. ViSOR operates by optimizing the
resolution for each target bitrate, given a maximum latency
threshold. The random forest algorithm is used to predict the
quality achieved after applying super-resolution, eliminating
redundant representations that do not improve perceptual
quality past the JND threshold. In this way, the encoding
energy is reduced significantly while remaining within
acceptable latency bounds and maintaining the perceptual
quality. However, such implementations assume that client
devices have GPU capabilities and are able to carry out real-
time super-resolution, which is certainly not always the case.
Nonetheless, it is a first step in the direction of energy- and
bandwidth-efficient video coding.
B. SUPER-RESOLUTION
Super-resolution (SR) is the process of enhancing the
resolution of an image to construct a high resolution
(HR) image from the one of low resolution (LR). In the
case of video applications, the ‘‘images’’ refer to the
video frames instead. Single-image super-resolution (SISR)
involves reconstructing HR video frames on an individual
basis. Video super-resolution (VSR), on the other hand,
also leverages temporal information across consecutive
frames to improve the super-resolution accuracy. As such,
SR techniques present an effective solution for improving
visual quality in image and video applications. This means
that they can also be leveraged to reduce video traffic. Rather
than requesting high video bitrates directly from the video
server, SR techniques can be utilized to enhance the quality
of low-quality chunks after transmission, thus reducing the
bandwidth required for streaming. The potential of this
technique has motivated a significant amount of research in
recent years, including industry-driven research efforts, such
as Amazon’s SUPERVEGAN [79]. In this section, we explore
the types of SR used for video streaming, as well as their
drawbacks and challenges.
1) SINGLE-FRAME SUPER-RESOLUTION (SFSR)
Several works use SISR for video quality enhancement,
as described above [80], [81], [82], [83]. In this method,
each video frame is enhanced individually, so it is sometimes
also referred to as per-frame or single-frame SR. Well-known
SISR models often used in the literature include SRCNN [84],
ESPCN [85], RDN [86], RFDN [87], and ESRGAN [88].
When used for video super-resolution, such methods have
the potential to greatly reduce the bandwidth required for
video streaming. In an earlier work that utilizes SFSR,
Yeo et al. [80] design a content-aware scheme that trains
several SR networks for different genres of video content.
Through the use of content-aware SR, their framework
achieves significant bandwidth savings while maintaining
perceptual quality.
While SFSR has a simpler processing pipeline com-
pared to multi-frame super-resolution, applying a super-
resolution model to every frame independently can become

non-key frames at a high resolution. Thus, the transmission

trains several video-specific overfitting neural networks of
different sizes.

Despite the immense potential of SR for simultaneously
improving video quality and reducing streaming traffic,
it faces several challenges, of which perhaps the most critical
is its high computational requirements. Most SR methods
use deep neural networks, such as CNNs and transformers,
which involve numerous parameters and require heavy com-
putations like matrix multiplications. As such, client devices
are often incapable of implementing real-time SR, especially
those devices that do not have GPU capabilities [95]. The
advent of edge computing has provided a potential solution to
this issue; edge servers have far more advanced capabilities,
making them suitable for carrying out computationally
expensive tasks such as SR.
Taking advantage of edge computing, many recent works
implement SR at the edge server [83], [96], [97], [98],
[99], [100], [101], [102]. Reference [96] presents a DRL-

the mobile edge computing (MEC) server to reduce the
computational load on the client. Another approach presented
by Filho and Melo [97] uses SR at the edge server to upscale
LR videos and store them along with the generated SR videos.
A generative adversarial network (GAN) is used to perform
SR in this work. GANs consist of a generative network-
which is trained to generate SR images from LR images
and reduce the difference between the SR and HR ground
truth images as far as possible- and a discriminator network-
which is trained to differentiate between synthetic SR frames
and HR frames. One noteworthy work that leverages edge-
assisted SR is presented in VISCA [98]. VISCA is a joint
SR and caching scheme that makes caching decisions based
on video popularity and potential SR enhancement. It then
enhances the quality of low quality cached content at the
edge using FRVSR [103]. In order to account for availability
of edge resources, VISCA’s ABR algorithm selects the
next segment resolution and decides whether to retrieve the
segment from the origin server or edge cache, or to apply
super-resolution to a LR version.
However, such edge-based approaches will naturally
increase the demand on the edge server and may waste
the computational capacity of more high-end devices. As
such, practical deployment of such edge-assisted SR schemes
remains scarce. This is addressed in Liu et al.’s work in [100].
Their work takes advantage of both edge server and client
computing resources to perform super-resolution, with a
DRL-based ABR algorithm that selects the video resolution,
reconstructed resolution, and client workload share. In their
framework, the server uses a large SR model to reconstruct
video tiles with low PSNR, while high PSNR video tiles are
reconstructed by a lightweight SR model at the client-side.

depends on the streamer’s network conditions. To address
this issue, the authors develop LiveNAS [104], a server-side
online super-resolution live video framework that enhances

5) VIDEO SUPER-RESOLUTION CHALLENGES
Although significant strides have been made in the field of
super-resolution, video super-resolution still faces numerous
challenges that stand in the way of widespread implementa-
tion. Device-dependence is one such factor that significantly
reduces the effectiveness of existing SR techniques. While
several SR models, such as ESPCN [85], are described as
low-latency, low-computation models with real-time perfor-
mance, it is important to clarify their feasibility on devices
without GPUs, such as mobile phones. SR performance
depends on several factors, including the input resolution.
On CPU-only devices, near real-time SR may be achieved for
low-resolution inputs. However, for higher-resolution inputs,
real-time SR is often not achievable due to the increased
number of computations required.
We illustrate this by using ESPCN to enhance the videos
of the Waterloo Streaming QoE Database-III [105] at three
input resolutions {144, 240, 360}p. We use an upscale factor
of 2 and measure the SR processing time in frames per
second (fps). The SR model achieves frame rates of 13.2-
18.5, 10.3-12.0, and 5.0-6.4 fps at the three input resolutions,
respectively. It can be seen that even input videos with
resolutions as low as 360p are still unable to achieve real-
time performance using state-of-the-art SR models without
GPU. This shows the need for SR techniques designed
specifically for CPU-only devices. Recently, NERVE [106]
shows great promise in implementing real-time VSR on
mobile devices. Other techniques are also proposed to
make SR more accessible for computationally-constrained
devices, such as early-exit schemes [107], which enable the
dynamic adaptation of SR network depth based on available
computational resources, and peer-to-peer (P2P) approaches,
such as that proposed by OASIS [108]. In OASIS, user
devices share the computational load of video enhancement.
OASIS’ algorithm jointly selects the video bitrate and SR
model to be used, then each device is assigned SR tasks.
Once video chunks are successfully enhanced, they can be
shared with other devices, thus improving the QoE without
additional computation.
Alternatively, recent research [109], [110] suggests the use
of image enhancement models that target artifacts, noise, and
compression losses rather than upscaling resolution. Refer-
ence [109] compares the performance of image enhance-
ment and SR techniques, finding that client-side image
enhancement improves the PSNR and reduces computation
time compared to SR. Thus, such methods may prove more
suitable for streaming on devices with limited computational
capabilities.
Moreover, enhancement-unaware ABR frameworks present
another challenge for SR-assisted streaming schemes. Most
of the current literature designs VSR models independently
from the ABR algorithm, where video enhancement is
not accounted for during bitrate selection. This results in
wasted bandwidth, as ABR algorithms may request chunks
with unnecessarily high bitrates when SR resources are
available. Therefore, integration of SR or image enhancement
with ABR algorithms is essential and has recently received
more attention in research [83], [102], [106], [107], [109],
[110], [111], [112]. For example, SuperABR [83] is a
recent edge-based framework combining bitrate adaptation
with quality enhancement. It utilizes a queue-learning-based
DRL approach to optimize both the source transmission
resolution and the VSR-reconstructed resolution, thus
balancing video quality and resource constraints. However,
such works remain few in the literature, leaving room for
improvement in the design of optimized enhancement-aware
ABR algorithms.
In addition, despite the proliferation of VSR methods,
quantifying the effect of SR on enhanced videos remains
a challenge, with few accurate quantitative measures and
evaluation frameworks. Some recent works have begun to
address this gap, such as Reznik et al.’s [113] SR quality
evaluation metric, which uses a generalized Westerink-
Roufs model. The authors in [114] also develop MoViDNN,
an evaluation application for video enhancement models on
mobile devices. This platform measures various performance
metrics, such as the PSNR, SSIM, and execution time,
allowing quality enhancement methods to be evaluated and
compared in a systematic way.
C. CACHING
The final bandwidth optimization technique discussed in this
work is known as caching, a method by which content is
copied and stored in a cache for quick retrieval. In order
to reduce the pressure on backhaul networks and minimize
delay, copies of video content are cached at edge servers to
bring them closer to the end user. However, edge servers have
limited storage capabilities. Cached content must therefore be
selected correctly to ensure that only popular videos that are
likely to be requested by many users are cached in order to
maximize bandwidth efficiency.
Caching schemes can be classified into two types: reactive
and proactive. Traditional reactive caching schemes select
cached content based on only local historic request patterns.
For example, the Least Recently Used (LRU) caching
technique replaces cached content that has not been requested
for the longest time once the server reaches its storage
capacity, which might lead to caching unpopular content
just because it has recently received requests. Another
reactive caching scheme is the Least Frequently Used (LFU)

Intuitively, the most obvious method of predicting content
popularity is through the use of historical streaming patterns.
Based on past popularity statistics, the future popularity of
video content can be predicted using learning techniques.
Some of the most popular learning methods used for this
task include long short-term memory (LSTM) [115] and
clustering algorithms for time-evolving engagement metrics,
such as k-means and canopy clustering [116]. Alternatively,
a deep learning approach is described in [117], where Li et al.

recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to extract information
about popular content based on user requests.

assumes that future popularity trends closely resemble past
behavior. However, the use of historical trends alone might
overlook emerging user demographics and new viral content.
To address this, Mao et al. present a caching scheme that
uses online reinforcement learning [118] and re-initializes the
RL agent at regular intervals. Meta-learning is then utilized
to reduce the convergence time. Mistakes and suboptimal
performance can still be seen during the convergence time
for such online algorithms, however.
2) CACHING BASED ON GEOGRAPHIC FEATURES
On the other hand, popular content varies not just temporally
but geographically as well. While temporal variations in
popularity capture how preferences evolve over time, geo-
graphic variations are influenced by factors such as cultural
differences, regional events, and localized user interests. For
example, sports highlights of a local event may receive
high demand in a specific region but very limited interest
elsewhere. This shows the need for caching techniques that
adapt to regional demand patterns.
To address this aspect, researchers have proposed the use
of caching frameworks that integrate geographic information.
The authors of [119] propose one such multi-agent DRL
framework for edge caching. Since one policy cannot be
shared across edges, the authors model each edge as an RL
agent. This approach also benefits from cooperation between
neighboring edges, which have similar content, so files
that are not cached at one edge are likely to be available
at a neighboring edge cache. Another interesting work is
presented by Zeng et al. [120], which proposes a caching
algorithm that uses users’ spatio-temporal context to track
content demand.
However, while using spatio-temporal context adds a
valuable dimension to caching decisions, accurately tracking
user mobility patterns requires access to real-time location
data, which raises potential concerns about privacy and data
security. Moreover, processing and analyzing such spatio-
temporal data can be computationally intensive, particularly
given the recent surge in video streaming traffic. As the
number of viewers and video requests grows, the data sent to
train the caching algorithm also increases correspondingly,
requiring powerful hardware at the edge, which may not
always be feasible. These challenges are addressed in [121],
which uses federated learning to shift training toward end-
user devices then aggregates the trained parameters at the
server. In addition to reducing the computational strain on
edge servers, this approach inherently enhances privacy,
as raw user data remains on local devices, and only the model
updates are shared with the server. Federated learning thus
provides a promising solution to scalability and privacy issues
in caching frameworks.

a user will request a specific video content. Content that is
likely to be highly requested by many users is then deemed
to be popular and can be cached proactively. In [123], the
historical request patterns of a user as well as request patterns
of similar users are used to predict the demand for video
content. Similarly, the authors in [124] develop a learning-
based collaborative filtering caching scheme that selects
cached content based on the similarity between users’ content
preferences as well as their location. Huang et al. [125] also

5) CACHING FOR LIVE SCENARIOS
Another application of learning techniques for traffic opti-
mization through caching involves live streaming, a rapidly
growing sector that imposes significant bandwidth demands.
Live streaming introduces several new problems to the
caching process; being latency-sensitive, live streams require
continuous updates and rapid cache replacement, increasing
the computational overhead. Moreover, Li et al. [128] study
mobile live streaming behaviors and statistics and conclude
that a large percentage of live video traffic is caused by
redundant uploads with no viewers. Additionally, they find
that live viewers tend to belong to the same locality with a
group of loyal viewers that download the most video data.
Therefore, to reduce the bandwidth wastage, the authors
propose EDGEOPT, which reduces the encoding rate of no-
viewer, unattractive uploads and uses learning-based pre-
fetching to cache the content of popular streams in each
region. They also design a peer-assisted video delivery
scheme that takes advantage of loyal viewers who tend to
watch streams for the longest duration and with greater
regularity. In case the edge server is facing high traffic,
the video content downloaded by loyal viewers can then
be shared across other viewers within the same region,
thus reducing the server load. Live streaming-related traffic
is also addressed by Ma et al. [129], leveraging edge
computing to aggregate client requests for the same video. By
processing these requests at the edge, their approach reduces
redundant transmissions and optimizes delivery efficiency.
However, many of these works have yet to address several
challenges associated with live content caching. For example,
live video viewership is often highly volatile, with some
streams experiencing sudden spikes in demand due to viral
moments or external events. Conventional caching methods
can struggle to effectively anticipate and react to these
fluctuations, leading to increased latency.

learning techniques to predict the optimal quality level

Dinaki et al. in [151]. Compared to other QoE prediction
schemes that use measured QoE metrics that may be outdated
by the time the ABR algorithm reacts, Dinaki et al.’s
forecasting scheme improves the prediction accuracy through
the use of bidirectional LSTMs (BiLSTMs). BiLSTMs
extract the bidirectional temporal dependencies of the
QoE metrics rather than only using past information for
prediction, thus increasing the model’s accuracy. However,
few works have focused on predicting QoE beyond the
current time step despite its potential to improve ABR
decision-making.
QoE degradation can also occur due to the misclassifica-
tion of user actions (such as pausing) as streaming events
(such as re-buffering). Casas et al. address this in their work
on DeepCrypt [152], which is a deep learning model for
QoE prediction that provides insight into user actions through
network traffic analysis; it differentiates between user actions
and streaming events with an accuracy of above 80%.
In another work [153], the authors use decision tree classifier
for inference of QoE metrics from encrypted video streams.
An alternative approach is presented in ExQoE [154]. Rather
than using objective metrics for QoE prediction, such as re-
buffering duration and average bitrate, ExQoE models QoE
based on users’ exiting behavior and models the user’s exiting
probability based on the video stalling occurrences. The QoE
model is then used to guide the selection of an appropriate
CDN and resolution using RL.
Other research focuses on developing QoE models that
more accurately reflect the characteristics of video content.
Although most of the literature uses bitrate as a quality
measure, bitrate alone cannot be used as a visual quality
metric due to the diversity of chunk complexity. In [155],
the authors investigate the relationship between chunk
complexity and quality. They find that most ABR algorithms
do not select sufficiently high bitrates for complex or dynamic
chunks, although achieving higher quality for dynamic
chunks than static ones leads to greater QoE gains. To handle
this issue, they design DAVS, a quality-aware apprenticeship
learning-based (refer to Table 1 for definitions of learning
techniques) scheme with a modified QoE function that
takes chunk complexity into account, as well as users’
QoE preferences. Reference [156] also presents a content-
aware QoE metric to guide the client’s bitrate selection,
based on video quality, playback fluency, volatility, as well
as video content features extracted using 3D CNN and
LSTM. The authors study the effects of each of these
factors on the accuracy of QoE prediction and find that
the accuracy is highest when a model combining all four
factors is used. However, due to the deep spatial-temporal
feature extraction and regression model, their model is highly
complex.
Other QoE prediction methods attempt to use Quality-of-
Service (QoS) metrics to predict the QoE. Mustafa et al. [157]
design an ABR algorithm-agnostic, ML-based QoE predic-
tion scheme based on QoS metrics, such as round-trip time
(RTT), number of packets per segment, and throughput.
Decision tree regression (DTR), multi-linear regression
(MLR), and random forest regression (RFR) are compared
in terms of prediction accuracy, and RFR is found to have the
highest QoE prediction accuracy. Another approach in [158]
uses a dueling DQN, which separates the state value function
and action advantage function. The adaptation algorithm is
carried out from the MEC server, where the transmission
bandwidth and the buffer occupancy are tracked. The video is
then transcoded at the edge server at the quality determined
by the adaptation algorithm.

B. QoE OPTIMIZATION FOR VoD
Reinforcement learning techniques are used predominantly
for adaptive bitrate selection. In a reinforcement learning-

the best possible action (i.e., the bitrate or quality level) given
a certain state (i.e., environmental factors, such as bandwidth
availability). The RL agent then learns a policy by which it
decides which actions will have the most favorable outcome,
as determined by the QoE. During training, this policy is
updated with each new experience learned by the agent.
A variety of RL-based ABR techniques have been developed
recently, particularly for VoD streaming, for several appli-
cations, such as conventional VoD streaming [166], energy-
aware streaming [167], and short video streaming [168],
among others. These algorithms are generally evaluated
based on achieved visual quality (indicated by metrics such
as VMAF) and overall QoE, with respect to a set of baseline
ABR models. There are several common models used in the
literature as a benchmark, including conventional [13], [14],

C. MODEL GENERALIZABILITY
Despite impressive performance shown by learning-based
ABR algorithms in the literature, in reality, ensuring that a
model performs consistently across diverse network environ-
ments remains a significant challenge. While heuristic ABR
algorithms can show robust behavior in unseen environments
due to their deterministic nature, learning-based algorithms
often struggle to generalize. This is mainly because RL
models are trained within specific environments in which
overfitting may occur. In machine learning, overfitting occurs
when models learn specific patterns in the training data too
well, leading to performance degradation when faced with
new, unseen conditions outside their training distribution.
Consequently, improving the generalizability of learning-
based ABR algorithms is a critical area of research that is
being tackled using a variety of techniques.
While offline learning algorithms may perform well in
environments included in their training, their performance
rapidly declines in new environments. In RL-based ABR,
once the network environment changes, the offline neural
network must be re-trained once again. The problem of
QoE degradation in learning-based ABR algorithms is
addressed in several works using online learning methods.
For example, the authors in [191] suggest using online
RL to update the neural network in real time if the QoE
drops below a predefined threshold. ABRaider [192] is
another approach that combines offline and online training
to balance generalizability and adaptability. In its offline
phase, a generalized neural network learns to select one of
five existing ABR algorithms based on expected rewards,

with several actors and a meta-critic that supervises and

learns to select one out of at least three DRL sub-policies

framework, meta-learning is used to transfer knowledge
across all sub-policies and speed up training. Similarly,
A2BR [199] uses meta-RL with a two-phase training process,
leveraging domain knowledge to improve adaptability to
client-specific network conditions. However, despite their
advantages, meta-learning approaches often require large
training datasets and computational resources, which presents
a barrier to their practical deployment.
Federated learning is another recent promising solution
for ABR generalizability. It differs from meta-learning in its
focus; meta-learning aims to improve a model’s ability to
quickly adapt to new tasks or environments by leveraging
knowledge from previous tasks. Federated learning, on the
other hand, trains models across distributed devices or
clients without centralizing data to ensure data privacy. This
is demonstrated in FedABR [200], which trains a global
model across multiple clients without sharing raw data, thus
ensuring privacy and allowing the creation of personalized
models for each client. However, federated learning has
its own limitations which inhibit its deployment in real-
world systems. Although this approach addresses privacy
concerns and computational bottlenecks at servers, it relies
on the availability of sufficient computational power on end-
user devices, which is not always guaranteed. Moreover,
federated learning primarily focuses on learning a single task
and produces a common global model. In contrast, meta-
learning can better adapt to heterogeneity, which makes it
more suitable in scenarios where personalization is necessary.

E. LIVE STREAMING
In previous sections, we have generally discussed ABR
algorithms designed for conventional streaming scenarios,
i.e., scenarios that are not latency-sensitive. In this section,
we now discuss low-latency algorithms for live streaming
scenarios. Due to the recent proliferation of live content,
particularly user-generated content, several works look into
QoE prediction and improvement for such scenarios as
well [211], [212], [213]. Live streaming presents several
unique challenges compared to conventional streaming due
to its stringent requirements for low latency and synchroniza-
tion. Unlike on-demand streaming, where slight buffering
delays can be acceptable, live streaming necessitates real-
time delivery to maintain viewer engagement, particularly in
the case of interactive video, which makes ABR algorithms
for live streaming particularly difficult to design.
Since maintaining latency within an acceptable range is
crucial for ensuring high QoE in live streaming, buffer
size is a key factor to consider: excessively large buffers
increase delay, while buffers that are too small might hinder
the delivery of high-quality segments. This has motivated
the development of buffer management techniques, such
as TCLiVi [214], which uses DRL to adaptively select
both the video bitrate and target buffer size. In addition,
several other approaches can also be used to reduce the
latency, such as frame skipping [215], [216], [217] and
playback speed adaptation [217], [218], [219]. The authors
in [215] use an RL framework for live ABR to reduce
live latency by using frame skipping when the latency rises
above a specified threshold. However, their approach does
not guarantee a high QoE, especially when the available
bandwidth is unstable or limited. Similarly, Deeplive [216]
is a Double-DQN [220] approach designed for live scenarios
which uses frame skipping to reduce latency. A quick-start
mechanism is also developed to use a rate-based algorithm
rather than Deeplive’s learning-based algorithm at the start
of the video stream, when historical state information is not
available yet. On the other hand, the work in [218] proposes a
low-latency DRL framework for selecting video quality and
playback speed for live streaming. This method maximizes
QoE given a live latency target and, rather than skipping
frames, slightly varies the playback speed to reduce latency.
Alternatively, the approach in [217] uses actor-critic DRL for
live scenarios to combine both frame skipping and playback
speed adaptation.
Live video streaming also benefits from peer-to-peer
systems. Recent research introduces such decentralized
streaming techniques in order to improve live stream quality,
reduce server loads, and tackle high video traffic during live
events. One notable work utilizing this concept is presented
in [221], which introduces a P2P system for crowdsourced
live video streaming. This scheme leverages a multiflow
framework that allows multiple parents to transmit redundant
video content to a single child viewer, ensuring uninterrupted
playback even if some parents leave the session. A DRL-
based scheduler is designed for multiflow transmission to
adaptively balance flow volumes and optimize the video
quality in real-time.
Given the more stringent latency requirements of live
streaming, accurately predicting the QoE of live videos
requires real-time quality assessment with high precision.
As such, live QoE prediction is another challenge that recent
research attempts to address, such as Vega et al.’s [213]
design of an unsupervised learning method involving the
use of Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBM) for online
live video quality assessment. However, the use of ML
techniques, particularly deep learning, remains a challenge
in live scenarios, so there are yet few studies suggesting its
use for QoE prediction, unlike VoD-focused research.
F. CONTENT-AWARE ADAPTATION
Video content also plays a role in determining users’
QoE. Much of the recent research is devoted to devel-
oping adaptation algorithms tailored to viewers’ content
preferences [228], [229]. In [229], the authors propose
an adaptation scheme that takes into account the user’s
preference in terms of video affective, or emotional, content
(e.g., joy, sadness, disgust, surprise, fear, and anger). Deep
learning is used to learn the user’s affective content (AC)
preferences offline from recent viewing history. Buffering
time is then allocated based on AC relevancy, so higher
affective segments receive higher quality representations.
The work in [225] also uses content-aware adaptation to
improve user QoE. However, in this work, video content
is classified based on scene type (e.g., dance, news, music,
etc.) rather than emotions. The user’s viewing history is
used to determine their preferences, and term frequency-
inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) is applied to ensure
that generally common and frequently occurring scenes are
not included in the viewer’s content preferences. In [228],
video content features are extracted by applying a 3D CNN
to 16 frames from each segment. DQL is then used for
rate adaptation based on the interestingness of each video
segment. Another recent RL-based work on content-aware
adaptation is presented in [226], which uses the number
of replay times to determine scene importance. However,
this method is not applicable for newly uploaded videos as
they would not yet have replay information. An interesting
alternative approach proposed by Ye et al. uses DRL to design
a visual sensitivity-aware adaptation algorithm [223].

whereas some are simple or static (e.g., interviews). Although
complex scenes have a larger impact on user QoE, they
tend to be requested at lower bitrates by ABR algorithms

V. POSSIBLE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
A. EMERGING TRENDS AND TECHNOLOGIES
Our study has identified several trends in current adaptive
streaming research, such as the popularity of reinforcement
learning methods, although some RL techniques, such as
Q-learning, have seen less recent interest. However, while
DRL methods, particularly the A3C method that has been
used in Pensieve [16], dominate current ABR research, future
research should further explore meta- and federated learning
approaches to meet the demand for increasingly user-centric
and personalized streaming.
In addition, although complex tasks such as bitrate
selection see limited use of shallow and supervised learning
techniques- due to label sparsity, temporal dependencies, and
high-dimensional action spaces- the scalability and robust-
ness of deep learning methods across real-time scenarios
remain lacking. This is because live streaming demands
less computationally intensive techniques, leaving heuristic
methods or shallow learning as a possible solution in
future ABR research. Similarly, in the case of caching,
collaborative filtering and other user-centric methods have
inspired the use of clustering algorithms to improve cache
content prediction. Video encoding is also another application
in which the use of shallow learning can provide several
benefits. This is motivated by the fact that video encoding is
a computationally hungry process, calling for less complex
techniques to reduce its energy and time demands; it also
typically involves less complex decision spaces compared
to bitrate selection, making shallow learning more feasible.
The random forest and decision tree algorithms in particular
have shown great promise in this field, although much
of previous research has focused on deep learning to the
exclusion of other alternatives. In the remainder of this
section, we further analyze recent trends in ML-based video
streaming.

efficiency and computational scalability, shifting from the
use of computationally intensive exhaustive bitrate ladder

provide further improvements in bandwidth-efficiency and
perceptual quality by incorporating contextual information

in highly dynamic environments. Future research should
explore real-time network modeling and prediction integrated
with encoder decision logic, in order to allow encoding
parameters to be adapted on-the-fly.
2) PERSONALIZED VIDEO STREAMING
There has been significant headway in accommodating
user preferences in terms of content and QoE in VoD
streaming. User preference-aware ABR algorithms that tailor
content delivery to individual user content preferences
(for example, based on genre) and QoE preference (by
prioritizing one or more QoE metrics over others) have
been discussed in Section IV. This aligns with ongoing
efforts to ensure that users receive the best possible perceived
quality for video streams. Recent studies have explored RL-
based personalization where policies adapt to user-specific
reward models. In VoD scenarios, where pre-analysis of
video content is feasible, personalization techniques such as
scene content analysis and emotion-based adaptation can be
implemented to enhance viewer engagement and satisfaction.
However, we observe that such personalized approaches are
comparatively scarce in live scenarios. Due to low-latency
requirements, implementing techniques such as content
analysis or quality enhancement becomes more challenging
in live scenarios. As such, personalized streaming remains a
focus mostly in VoD applications. Other challenges include
the cold-start problem, in which new users or content with
no prior information inhibit the operation of personalized
streaming systems; privacy risks when analyzing user data;
and the complexity of multi-objective reward design for
personalized user-centric QoE models.
3) 5G FOR LOW-LATENCY STREAMING
The deployment of 5G networks introduces higher band-
width and low latency, which significantly benefit video
streaming applications. Features such as network slicing
(which allows isolation of streaming workloads on dedicated
virtual network slices), massive MIMO (which increases
spectral efficiency), and millimeter-wave (mmWave) com-
munication (which offers high data rates over short distances)
provide resources for high-throughput, delay-sensitive video
streaming. Within this context, machine learning models
are now increasingly being used to design 5G-based ABR
algorithms. This is because, despite their potential, 5G
networks suffer from several challenges that limit their
real-life performance, such as signal blockage in mmWave,
dynamic user mobility, fluctuating link quality, and energy
consumption at base stations. ML-based algorithms are thus
being proposed to address these issues through techniques
such as proactive adaptation, chunk prefetching, and buffer
management [74], [222], [235], [236], [237]. However, most
current adaptation algorithms are still not designed to exploit
5G-specific features. Integrating ABR models with functions
such as 5G network prediction remains a largely untapped
area.
4) USER BEHAVIOR ANALYSIS
When watching video content, viewers do not always require
the highest possible quality that their network conditions
can support. Often, users play videos in the background
while doing something else (for example, playing a music
video in another tab or watching a movie on a separate
screen while working). Some short video applications, such
as TikTok or YouTube Live, also involve scrolling through
videos rapidly, with users often skimming through to find
their preferred content without focusing on the videos.
In these cases, a typical quality adaptation algorithm would
still continue to deliver the video at the highest possible
quality, even though the user is not actively watching, leading
to bandwidth wastage. With the development of device
capabilities that enable users to play videos at a high quality
even while using the device for other tasks, as well as the
growing popularity of short video applications, streaming
algorithms that take user behaviors into account have recently
been developed to improve bandwidth efficiency. Techniques
to track user engagement and streaming behavior using
ML are gaining attention in research. Recent research
into short video streaming provides several basic solutions,
including predictive video pre-loading based on user behavior
patterns. However, this introduces the question of how
streaming systems can detect and adapt to user engage-
ment levels and interaction patterns in real time without
violating users’ privacy. Future architectures may rely on
decentralized schemes such as federated models, but these
methods are still in their infancy within the field of video
streaming.

5) LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS FOR VIDEO APPLICATIONS
Recent years have witnessed the emergence of several signif-

challenges. High computational overhead, memory demands,

for practical deployment in video streaming applications.

Edge computing is another technology that is now generating
immense interest in multimedia streaming. Edge servers help
reduce backhaul congestion and latency as they are closer
to end users, making them essential for real-time streaming
applications. Additionally, they have much higher computing
capabilities, allowing them to carry out computationally
intensive tasks, such as training learning models or applying
video enhancement.

attention over the past decade, there are still several open
opportunities. First, the coordination and optimization of
multi-edge environments provides an open area of research
for enhanced video streaming. For instance, dynamic task
offloading, which determines which enhancement or infer-
ence operations should be performed at the client, edge,
or cloud, remains an open challenge. The scheduling of super-
resolution or VQA tasks across resource-constrained edge
devices introduces a new avenue for edge-assisted adaptive
streaming. Such approaches could ensure that the capabilities
of both clients and edge servers are utilized effectively
without overloading the edge server, a common problem
in edge-assisted schemes. Additionally, cooperative caching
across multiple edge nodes to optimize cache placement for
both high-demand content and enhancement models remains
underexplored.
B. PRACTICAL DEPLOYMENT CHALLENGES
Despite remarkable advances in the field of video streaming,
we observe that there remains a gap between current research
practices and real-world streaming applications, particularly
in the case of machine learning-based frameworks. This
manifests in several aspects, such as the impractically
high computational requirements of many ML solutions,
especially those leveraging DNNs. Many devices are unable
to implement such computationally demanding and energy-
consuming operations. Recent research has therefore focused
on providing solutions to reduce the complexity of ML
algorithms and make them more scalable, using techniques
such as early-exit models [81], [107]. A notable recent study
has also proposed the interpretation of DNN models to
equivalent decision tree models, which are far less complex,
with nearly identical performance [239].
Learning-based algorithms also suffer from an inability
to perform well in real streaming environments. These
learning-based algorithms are typically trained and tested
in simulated environments, which are unable to accurately
mimic real-world streaming scenarios. As such, trained
models show good performance in simulated environments
but perform dismally in real ones, making their practical
deployment challenging. Some research has discussed the
so-called simulated-real streaming gap [18], illustrating the
lack of adaptability to real-world conditions of learning-
based algorithms. Projects such as Puffer [19] have recently
been implemented to address this challenge, yet the issue
remains for most state-of-the-art algorithms due to the lack
of comprehensive learning-based training platforms using
real-world data. To bridge this simulated-real streaming gap,
future research should utilize testbeds and datasets with
real network conditions rather than relying on training and
evaluation within simulated environments. In addition, online
training techniques, which allow learning models to be fine-
tuned using live feedback, can be integrated in learning-based
streaming systems to improve their real-world performance.
In addition, the lack of end-to-end streaming frameworks is
another challenge that hinders the integration of ML in real-
world systems. Aspects such as computational complexity,
latency, and device compatibility must be considered on
the system-level, rather than component-level. We have
touched upon this topic in Section IV-A, discussing the lack
of research integrating QoE prediction models with other
components of the streaming system. More comprehensive
streaming frameworks remain scarce in the current literature,
with few exceptions in the HAS domain [167], [204] and real-
time communications [240]. Designing a holistic pipeline
that jointly learns encoding decisions, quality adaptation, and
enhancement under real-world constraints requires address-
ing several open questions. First, task-specific optimization
must be balanced with overall QoE gain. For instance,
ABR policies that overly prioritize rebuffering may reduce
perceptual quality when not aligned with super-resolution
timing. Second, such end-to-end frameworks must allow
modular retraining and A/B testing without retraining the
entire pipeline. Therefore, implementing such frameworks
requires collaboration across systems and design of multi-
objective models, where LLMs are now being suggested
as a possible solution. The OpenNetLab [241] and Arse-
nal [242] frameworks, although developed for real-time
communications (RTC), also offer a promising blueprint to

on privacy-preserving architectures tailored specifically for
video streaming contexts, with accurate, secure, and real-time

can reconstruct private user data from model outputs,
and adversarial attacks, which can use defective inputs
to mislead ML-based streaming models. To address these

promising solution for enhancing the security, transparency,
and decentralization of ML systems. For example, immutable
ledgers can ensure integrity and traceability of training data
and model updates, while smart contracts can enforce access
control over user data or encoded streams. Consequently,

stronger guarantees of data integrity and user privacy,

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper provides a comprehensive review of recent
advancements in the application of machine learning for
HTTP adaptive video streaming. It examines the chal-
lenges faced by existing streaming systems and outlines
the potential advantages of employing machine learning
algorithms to overcome these obstacles. The paper cat-
egorizes and compares machine learning-based methods
for video encoding, bandwidth optimization, and quality
adaptation. It also highlights several promising directions
for future research. While impressive advances have been
made in simulated environments, real-world deployment
continues to be hindered by generalization issues and
practical constraints of latency, privacy, complexity, and
energy consumption. Looking forward, research must target
the design of lightweight, adaptive ML models tailored for
heterogeneous devices and the creation of privacy-preserving
architectures for user-centric streaming. Additionally, there
is an urgent need for standardized evaluation benchmarks
and end-to-end frameworks that unify diverse streaming
components. By addressing these challenges, the next
generation of learning-based video streaming systems can
become more intelligent, personalized, and ready for real-
world deployment.
